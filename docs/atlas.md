# FH Atlas: Semantic Instrumentation of the Mathematical Search Space

**Status:** Draft 0.2 (amended 2026-08-01) · Companion to design doc, corpus review, and agent layer.
**Thesis:** FH's differentiating layer is not proving (a solved-elsewhere arms race) but *read-only semantic analysis* of elaborated environments — ours plus Mathlib's ~420k declarations (≈135k definitions + ≈284k theorems as of mid-2026) — exposed to agents as queryable structure: what depends on what, what is secretly the same shape as what, what generalizes, what transports, and where the unexplored interfaces between theories lie. Everything here consumes `Environment` data (`ConstantInfo`: every declaration's type and proof term as `Expr` trees) via a Lean-side extractor; the index/query engine lives in Rust, where it belongs.

The conceptual foundation is the "dictionary" machinery from the project's origin: a dictionary between theories is a partial structure-preserving map; proven rows transport theorems; missing entries localize research. The Atlas mechanizes each clause.

## 1. The five indexes

**1a. Dependency graph (the citation layer).** Nodes: declarations. Edges: "proof of X uses Y," extracted by walking proof terms — not imports, actual term-level usage. Cheap, exact, and the substrate for everything else. Derived metrics per node: transitive foundation (what it rests on), transitive impact (what rests on it), and *bridge centrality* — declarations whose removal disconnects theory clusters are the load-bearing walls, and agents should know a wall when they lean on one.

**1b. Bound-minimization index (the generalization layer).** For each theorem, the delta between *stated* hypotheses and *consumed* hypotheses. Statement says `CommRing R`; replay the proof term against the instance lattice and discover multiplication's commutativity is never invoked — the theorem is really about `Ring`, stated too strong. Mechanism: for typeclass bounds, attempt re-elaboration of the proof term under the weakened instance, walking down the hierarchy lattice until elaboration fails; the last success is the theorem's **true home**. (For plain hypotheses, term-walking suffices: an unused `h` never appears in the proof term — Mathlib's linter already catches this shallow case; the lattice-walk is the deep version.) The lattice-walk as stated operates on theorems that already carry class binders; for *concrete-instance* theorems (unique factorization over ℤ) it is preceded by a **carrier-abstraction** step — replace the concrete carrier with a fresh variable, collect the instance obligations the proof term actually demands, then walk down from there. This abstract-then-minimize composite is what validation target V1 exercises when it asks for both factorization theorems' shared EuclideanDomain home. Every gap found is a free generalization — a new, strictly stronger theorem whose proof already exists. This is the conversation's "audit which properties of ℤ the proof actually consumed" as a batch job over all of Mathlib, and to my knowledge nobody runs it systematically.

**1c. Skeleton index (the analogy layer).** The core novel structure. For each statement, compute a *skeleton*: the statement's `Expr` tree with concrete constants abstracted to variables labeled by their minimal governing structure (which 1b supplies — abstraction should be to the *true* requirement, not the stated one). The formal operation is **anti-unification** (Plotkin's least general generalization): given two terms, compute the most specific pattern that matches both. Two statements in different theories anti-unifying to a nontrivial skeleton are a **candidate dictionary row**. Examples the machinery must catch, all from our own conversation: unique factorization over ℤ and over `Polynomial (ZMod p)` anti-unify to the EuclideanDomain skeleton (a known row — sanity check); "eigenvalues of self-adjoint operators are real" and "zeros of ξ lie on the critical line" share a spectral-reality skeleton (the Hilbert–Pólya row — famously conjectural); "⟨Wf, f⟩ ≥ 0" and intersection-positivity on surfaces share a quadratic-form-positivity skeleton (the Weil-positivity row). Indexing: exact skeleton hashing for the fast path, embedding similarity as a recall-oriented prefilter, pairwise anti-unification as the precision pass on candidates. Embeddings alone are fuzzy vibes; anti-unification alone is O(n²) death; the hybrid is the design. (Budget the prefilter against n ≈ 420k statements — the corrected Mathlib count roughly quadruples the naive pairwise cost relative to earlier drafts' 200k figure.)

**1d. Equivalence graph (the reformulation layer).** Nodes: propositions and structures; edges: proven `Iff`s, `Equiv`s, and definitional unfoldings. Connected components are *reformulation clusters* — the machine-readable version of "RH is equivalent to Λ ≤ 0, to a positivity, to a growth bound on M(x)." Agents attacking a problem query its cluster first: every reformulation is a different attack surface, and the highest-value query in mathematics is often "state this differently." Cross-cluster near-misses (high skeleton similarity, no proven edge) are candidate equivalences — conjecture generation with a built-in verification path.

**1e. Proof-shape index (the strategy layer).** Index proofs, not statements: tactic-sequence skeletons and dependency motifs ("reduces to a positivity," "induction on a monovariant," "minimal-criminal," "shuffle-the-deck bijection" — the named moves from our axioms discussions, now as searchable patterns). Query: "what proof shapes succeed against statements with skeleton S?" This turns proof strategy from folklore into retrieval, and it is exactly what an agent lacks when staring at a fresh goal: not lemmas (Loogle/LeanSearch retrieve those) but *approaches*.

## 2. The operations

**`atlas similar <decl>`** — skeleton-index lookup: statements structurally rhyming with this one, ranked, cross-theory matches boosted (same-theory rhymes are usually boring siblings; the value density is in cross-theory hits).

**`atlas home <decl>`** — 1b: the weakest structure where the proof survives, plus the hypothesis delta. Doubles as a debugging tool: when an agent's proof attempt fails, comparing the goal's true home against the available instances answers "am I even in the right theory?"

**`atlas dictionary <theoryA> <theoryB>`** — assemble the maximal partial functor: all skeleton-matched rows between two theory fragments, each tagged with its epistemic status (both proven / one proven / neither), plus the **missing-entry report**: concepts on one side with no matched partner. The number-field/function-field dictionary's missing Frobenius entry is the canonical example of what this report surfaces; the whole point is finding the *next* such gap in territory no human has mapped.

**`atlas transport <rows> <statement>`** — the active operation. Apply a dictionary's substitution to a statement, then dispatch the image through three checks: does it already exist (search)? does the falsification battery kill it (agent layer §3)? is it provable (hand to prover agents as a directed target)? All three outcomes are signal: existing = dictionary strengthened, one more verified row; refuted = **the analogy's boundary located** — as our conversation established with unique factorization failing identically in both number fields and function fields, where an analogy breaks is itself structural knowledge, and the Atlas records broken rows as first-class data; proven = a new theorem, generated by analogy and checked by kernel.

**`atlas frontier <theoryA> <theoryB>`** — negative-space detection: theory pairs with high aggregate skeleton similarity but near-zero dependency-graph cross-citation. High similarity + no traffic = unexplored interface. Ranked output is a research agenda.

**`atlas why <declA> <declB>`** — path queries over the union of all graphs: how is A connected to B (dependency chains, shared skeletons, equivalence paths, common ancestors in the instance lattice)? The "decompile the relationship" primitive, and the first thing an agent should run when a human asks "is X relevant to Y?"

## 3. The worked example: the corpus vs. RH

The demo required by the project's own mythology. Formalize RH's *statement* and its classical reformulations (statement-only — `sorry`-free statements need no proofs; DeepMind's Formal Conjectures repo demonstrates the genre), load the dedicated validation clusters of `atlas-validation.md` §2 (Spectral, Positivity, Deformation, FF-arithmetic, …) with the twelve corpus groups included as background noise/controls, and run the pipeline. What honest output looks like:

`atlas similar` on the RH cluster surfaces the Spectral cluster's spectrum-is-real statements through the spectral-reality skeleton (self-adjoint ⇒ real spectrum — the two-line proof from our linear-algebra discussion — anti-unifies with "nontrivial zeros have real part ½" *under the Hilbert–Pólya reading*, and the Atlas should report the match with its status: skeleton-identical, dictionary-row conjectural, which is precisely the informative answer). The Deformation cluster's monotone-deformation statements match the de Bruijn–Newman reformulation. The corpus groups play the control role: Group 12's monovariant/termination patterns must match nothing in the RH cluster — also correct, also reported. (Earlier drafts claimed corpus Groups 2/5 would fire the spectral match; they contain no spectral statements — group theory and rank–nullity respectively — which is exactly why the validation benchmark builds a dedicated Spectral cluster and demotes the corpus groups to controls.) `atlas transport` on the spectral row then generates the directed question "exhibit the inner-product space making the row real," which is... the actual open problem, rediscovered by machine from structure alone. That's the honest calibration of what this tool does: **it will not produce novel RH insight from a few hundred statement-level ingredients — it should *rediscover the known attack structure* from them, mechanically.** Rediscovery-of-known-structure is the validation benchmark (if the Atlas can't find Hilbert–Pólya when handed both sides, it can't find anything); novelty is a scale phenomenon, emerging when the same mechanism runs over 400k+ declarations where no human holds the whole graph — the regime where "nobody ever noticed these two lemmas rhyme" becomes statistically inevitable rather than lucky.

## 4. Prior art and the differentiator

Retrieval exists: Loogle (type patterns), LeanSearch/Moogle (semantic), LeanDojo's premise selection — all answer "find me a lemma," none answer "find me a relationship." Automated theory formation has a lineage (Lenat's AM, Colton's HR) that generated conjectures but drowned in noise for lack of grounding. Embedding-based clustering of theorem libraries exists and stops at fuzzy similarity. The Atlas's differentiator is the combination: *symbolic* skeletons (anti-unification, not just cosine similarity) over a *kernel-checked* corpus with a *verification-closed loop* (every proposed relationship is dispatchable to falsification or proof, so noise is self-limiting — the failure mode of every prior theory-formation system, hallucinated relationships, gets filtered by the same Lean kernel that makes the corpus trustworthy in the first place). FH's role metadata (design doc §4.9) feeds skeleton labeling; the agent layer (`atlas mcp`) is the delivery surface; Rust owns the graph and index engines. No component modifies the prover; the prover is the oracle that keeps the Atlas honest.

## 5. Build order

Dependency graph first (a weekend: term-walker + petgraph), because every other index consumes it and its queries (`why`, foundations, impact) are immediately useful for corpus debugging. Then bound-minimization over the corpus + a Mathlib slice (validates the lattice-walk mechanic on ground truth: it must rediscover known Mathlib generalization lint hits). Then skeletons + `similar` (the anti-unification engine — the hard, novel 20%). Equivalence graph and proof shapes ride on the same extraction pass. `transport`/`frontier`/`dictionary` are query-layer compositions once the indexes exist. Benchmark gate before any novelty claims: the Atlas must pass the rediscovery suite — EuclideanDomain row, Hilbert–Pólya row, Weil-positivity row, and at least one known Mathlib over-hypothesized lemma. (2026-08-01: per atlas-validation §1's amendment these named rows are development targets — legitimate for building against; the *cold* claim is earned by the held-out suite in the private answer key.)

## 6. Metadata over Mathlib without a fork

Mathlib is treated as an upstream binary: never rewritten, never annotated at source, overlaid. Three channels, by distance from Lean.

**Channel 1 — retroactive attributes and environment extensions (in-Lean, no source edits).** Lean permits applying attributes to declarations from *downstream* files: `attribute [fh_role ...] Nat.Prime.dvd_mul` in an FH package file attaches our metadata to a Mathlib declaration at import time, persisted via a custom environment extension into *our* olean. Anything importing FH sees Mathlib-with-metadata; Mathlib itself is untouched. This channel is for metadata that Lean-side elaboration must see (Bridge aliases, notation alignments, role tags that influence FH expansion). Caveats recorded (2026-08): FH-registered attributes and environment extensions are unrestricted, but retroactive application of *builtin* attributes is subject to Lean's same-module restriction on a minority of attributes (and not every attribute supports erasure); and Lean's new module system serializes environments into three olean visibility levels, so extension-data export behavior needs a compatibility check as Mathlib migrates to it.

**Channel 2 — the computed sidecar (out-of-Lean, the bulk).** Most Atlas data — skeletons, true homes, dependency edges, proof shapes — is *derived*, so it never needs to live in Lean at all: one extraction pass walks the elaborated environment and emits facts into the Rust-side store. **Decided (2026-08-01):** the first extractor is a minimal Lean metaprogram emitting JSONL rows (name, kind, used-constant list, statement-hash inputs) — sufficient for the dependency graph and the keying scheme below; full `lean4export`-style whole-`Expr` dumps are adopted only when the skeleton index (1c) needs complete terms. Incremental by module hash; recomputed on toolchain bumps. Derived data is rebuildable by construction, so staleness is a performance question, not a correctness one.

**Channel 3 — asserted overlay files (human/agent annotations).** An FH declaration form that emits no Lean code, only metadata rows: `annotate Nat::Prime::dvd_mul { shape: certificate_deployment, note: "Bézout multiplied through by b" }`. These are opinions about upstream declarations, versioned like patches against a moving target.

**The keying problem is the real design content.** Mathlib changes daily; names get renamed, statements get strengthened. Every metadata row is keyed by `(Name, statement-hash)` where the hash is over the elaborated `Expr` normalized up to alpha-equivalence and universe renaming — content addressing, so metadata survives renames (rebind by hash when a name moves; Mathlib's `@[deprecated]` alias chains cover most renames automatically) and *detects* statement changes (hash mismatch = the annotation is stale, flagged exactly like drifted source maps, never silently reattached). Asserted metadata (Channel 3) hard-fails on hash mismatch pending re-review; derived metadata (Channel 2) just recomputes. This is the debug-symbols discipline: the sidecar is only trustworthy because it can always tell when the binary underneath it changed.
